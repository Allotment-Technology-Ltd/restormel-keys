# A utilitarian objection to rights absolutism

Rights-based theories hold that certain actions are wrong even when they would produce greater overall good. A utilitarian critic objects that this position treats moral rules as free-floating constraints disconnected from human welfare.

Consider a public health emergency in which vaccinating everyone would prevent thousands of deaths. A rights absolutist might claim that mandatory vaccination violates bodily autonomy regardless of consequences. The utilitarian replies that respecting autonomy is valuable precisely because it generally promotes well-being, but rigid absolutism can produce catastrophic harm when stakes are extreme.

The rights theorist responds that treating persons as ends requires limits on what may be done to them, even for collective benefit. Utilitarianism, they argue, risks justifying oppression of minorities whenever a majority gains enough utility.

The utilitarian counter-objection is that long-run welfare itself depends on stable rights and trust. Therefore, a sophisticated utilitarian need not endorse every trade-off that naive aggregation suggests.

It follows that the debate is not simply "numbers versus rights," but competing accounts of which principles best protect persons over time. Both sides must explain how their view handles edge cases without collapsing into arbitrary intuition.
