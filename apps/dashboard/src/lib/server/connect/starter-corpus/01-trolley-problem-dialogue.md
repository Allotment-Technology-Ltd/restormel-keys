# The trolley problem — a dialogue

**Alex:** Suppose a runaway trolley will kill five people unless you pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, where one person will die instead. Is it permissible to pull the lever?

**Jordan:** Many people say yes. You cause one death but prevent five. The net outcome is better, so pulling the lever seems justified.

**Alex:** But consider a different case. You could push a large person off a bridge to stop the trolley. That also saves five lives at the cost of one. Is pushing permissible?

**Jordan:** That feels different. In the bridge case you use the person as a means. In the lever case you merely redirect an existing threat.

**Alex:** So your objection is not about the numbers alone. You distinguish between intending harm as a means and accepting harm as a side effect.

**Jordan:** Exactly. However, a strict utilitarian might reply that both actions maximize welfare, so moral difference is irrational.

**Alex:** A deontologist would counter that some constraints on how we treat persons are not reducible to aggregate outcomes. Therefore, permission to pull the lever does not automatically license pushing someone off the bridge.

**Jordan:** It follows that ethical theory must explain both our intuitions about cases and the principles that unify them. Without that, we have disconnected judgments rather than a coherent moral view.
