# Knowledge versus true belief

Plato's classic question asks what distinguishes knowledge from mere true belief. Suppose a juror believes the defendant is guilty, and the defendant is in fact guilty. If the juror reached that belief by lucky guess rather than reliable evidence, we hesitate to call it knowledge.

The standard response defines knowledge as justified true belief. A belief counts as knowledge when it is true, the believer has adequate justification, and the justification is of the right kind.

However, Gettier-style cases challenge this definition. Imagine an employee who justifiably believes they will get a promotion because their boss told them so. Unbeknownst to them, the boss was joking, yet a hidden committee had already decided to promote them. The employee's belief is true and justified, yet it seems not to be knowledge.

One reply holds that knowledge requires a reliable connection between belief and fact — what epistemologists call a safe or sensitive link. Another reply treats knowledge as a primitive state that cannot be fully analyzed into belief plus justification.

Therefore, epistemology must clarify not only when beliefs are true, but when they are appropriately connected to the facts. Without that clarification, agents cannot distinguish trustworthy claims from accidentally correct guesses.
